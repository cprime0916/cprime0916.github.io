# Exploring the urban renewal strategy based on transit-oriented development concept--A case study of Japan and Hong Kong

> [!summary] One-Sentence Positioning
> This paper systematically reviews the historical evolution and contemporary challenges of urban renewal, synthesizes core TOD principles (3D/5D), and demonstrates through case studies of Tokyo and Hong Kong (West Kowloon) how transit-oriented development enables land intensification, functional integration, pedestrian roaming, and environmental decarbonization in high-density urban renewal.

## 2. Research Background & Problem

### 2.1 Research Background
Amid decreasing urban development space in China, urban development is shifting from incremental expansion to stock renewal (PDF p. 1). Unorderly urban sprawl has caused land shortages and urban decay. In response, China's Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development (MOHURD) launched the "Implementation of Urban Renewal Action" in November 2020 and designated pilot cities in November 2021 (PDF p. 1). Rail transit acts as a vital catalyst for stock optimization, optimizing spatial patterns, driving internal regeneration, and integrating station-adjacent land (PDF p. 1).

### 2.2 Literature Gap
Traditional urban renewal literature often focuses on single physical transformations or isolated transport upgrades, failing to systematically integrate TOD logic into stock urban regeneration (PDF pp. 2–3). Existing practices frequently suffer from:
1. Fragmented land preparation and complex tenure disputes (PDF p. 3).
2. Car-oriented road widening that exacerbates traffic congestion rather than solving it (PDF p. 3).
3. Social inequity, economic segregation, and gentrification displacement of low-income groups (PDF p. 3).
4. Irreparable destruction of historical and cultural urban fabrics (PDF p. 4).
5. Erosion and compression of public spaces by private real estate developers seeking high return values (PDF p. 4).

### 2.3 Research Objectives & Research Questions
- **Research Objectives**: To analyze the historical stages of urban renewal, articulate the underlying logic and coordination mechanisms between TOD and urban renewal, and synthesize strategic characteristics via case studies from Japan and Hong Kong to guide future stock renewal efforts (PDF p. 1).
- **Research Questions**:
  1. How has urban renewal evolved historically, and what core challenges plague traditional renewal practices? (PDF pp. 2–4)
  2. How do TOD theoretical principles (3D/5D) coordinate with the objectives, stakeholder interests, and design concerns of urban renewal? (PDF pp. 4–5)
  3. What specific operational strategies (intensification, functional complexity, green roaming networks, decarbonization) are demonstrated in Japanese and Hong Kong TOD hubs? (PDF pp. 5–8)
- **Hypotheses / Propositions**: The authors do not state explicit formal hypotheses (PDF p. 1). [Analytical Inference] The underlying proposition is that adopting TOD principles in urban renewal resolves the conflicts of land scarcity, traffic congestion, and environmental degradation while optimizing economic and social value.

### 4.3 Methodological Process
1. **Literature Review & Typology Construction**: Systematized urban renewal history into four stages and classified five main contemporary challenges (PDF pp. 2–4).
2. **Theoretical Alignment Analysis**: Mapped 3D/5D TOD principles against urban renewal goals across three dimensions: concept, stakeholder interest, and physical design (PDF pp. 4–5).
3. **Empirical Case Study Evaluation**: Analyzed Tokyo's Shinjuku, Nihonbashi, and Shibuya developments alongside Hong Kong's West Kowloon Station to assess FAR scaling, functional mixing, and 3D pedestrian connectivity (PDF pp. 5–7).
4. **Environmental & Financial Cost-Benefit Comparison**: Evaluated rail transit's atmospheric emission reductions ($NO_x$, $CO_2$, $CO$, $HC$, $SO_2$) and modal energy efficiency relative to private automobiles and highway travel (PDF pp. 7–8).

## 5. Main Results

### 5.1 Core Findings
1. **Evolution to TOD Renewal Era**: Urban renewal has evolved from post-war bulldozer clearing (1950s) to resident self-built projects (1960s), community planning (1970s–80s), and finally to TOD-driven stock regeneration (1990s–present) (PDF pp. 2–3).
2. **Land Intensification & Floor Area Ratio (FAR) Escalation**: In high-density TOD renewal, land use transitions from horizontal sprawl to vertical multi-layered space. In Tokyo’s Nihonbashi 2-Chome redevelopment (4.8 hm²), the Special Urban Regeneration District framework achieved an average FAR of up to 14.0 (up from 7.55 baseline) (PDF p. 6).
3. **Multi-Layered Functional Integration (Shibuya Station)**: Modern TOD projects shift from single-function commercial hubs to integrated networked complexes. The Shibuya HIKARIE complex integrates subway lines, bus terminals, commercial retail, cultural exchange venues, and creative industry facilities (PDF pp. 6–7).
4. **Three-Dimensional Roaming & Public Space Creation (Hong Kong West Kowloon)**: Placing transit platforms underground increases upfront construction cost (Table 3) but generates vast public space benefits: West Kowloon Station created a 9,000 m² public square, 30,000 m² of green landscape, and an integrated elevated/underground pedestrian roaming network (PDF p. 7).
5. **Significant Environmental Decarbonization**: Rail transit consumes 347 kJ/person·km compared to 2,562 kJ/person·km for private automobiles (a 1:5.9 ratio) (PDF p. 8). Rail transit reduces $CO_2$ by 82% and $NO_x$ by 72% relative to highway travel, with every 100 million passenger increase in rail transit saving 31,000 tons of fuel (PDF pp. 7–8).

### 5.2 Secondary Findings & Anomalies
- **Underground Construction Value Dynamics**: In tunnel and underground hub excavation, decoration operations yield the highest value coefficient ($V_i = 2.875$), while initial excavation operations have a low value coefficient ($V_i = 0.375$) due to high initial capital outlay (Table 3, PDF p. 7).
- **Mobile Source Pollution Dominance**: In 2020, mobile sources accounted for 55.6% (5.669 million tons) of China's total national $NO_x$ emissions (10.197 million tons), surpassing industrial sources (417.5 ten thousand tons) (Figure 8, PDF pp. 7–8).

## 6. Discussion & Contributions

### 6.1 Theoretical Contributions
- Integrates traditional urban renewal taxonomy with modern 3D/5D TOD theoretical principles (PDF pp. 2–4).
- Formalizes a unified framework linking TOD station area enhancement to multi-stakeholder interest alignment and urban stock optimization (Figure 1, PDF p. 5).

### 6.2 Methodological Contributions
- Demonstrates the application of value engineering / cost-coefficient analysis (Table 3) to evaluate underground transit hub development trade-offs (PDF p. 7).

### 6.3 Empirical Contributions
- Provides empirical case evidence from Tokyo (Shinjuku, Nihonbashi, Shibuya) and Hong Kong (West Kowloon) detailing quantitative FAR increases, green surface areas (30,000 m²), and multi-modal emission factors (PDF pp. 5–8).

### 6.4 Practical or Policy Implications
- Policy makers should utilize "Special Urban Regeneration District" mechanisms to grant FAR incentives in exchange for public space, pedestrian roaming networks, and green infrastructure (PDF pp. 5–6).
- City authorities should prioritize underground/three-dimensional station hub designs to overcome surface land scarcity and restore urban pedestrian continuity (PDF p. 7).

## 7. Limitations & Future Research

### 7.1 Limitations Explicitly Acknowledged by Authors
- High initial economic costs associated with underground platform and tunnel excavation operations (Table 3, PDF p. 7).
- Structural complexities in land preparation and tenure replacement among multiple private and public property rights holders (PDF p. 3).

### 7.2 Further Identified Limitations
- **[Analytical Inference] Lack of Ecological & Biodiversity Metrics**: The study focuses almost exclusively on urban central built-up environments, high-density FAR exploitation, and urban core transport emissions, completely ignoring natural ecological constraints, biodiversity preservation, or wetland hydrology.
- **[Analytical Inference] Omission of Financial Over-reliance Risks**: The model assumes real estate land value capture will always offset transit construction costs, which may fail in secondary or peripheral sub-centers with weaker property markets.

### 7.3 Future Research Directions
- **Authors' Suggestions**: Application of full Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) tools during the project planning and implementation stages to quantify precise material use and environmental emissions (PDF p. 2).
- **[Analytical Inference] Recommended Direction**: Extending TOD framework evaluation to environmentally sensitive rural-urban fringe zones and ecologically protected areas where high-density real estate incentives conflict with conservation mandates.

## 9. Relationship with My Research

### Target Research Question
*What are the key environmental and structural limitations of Hong Kong’s traditional "Rail plus Property" (R+P) TOD framework when deployed in ecologically sensitive wetland ecosystems in the Northern Metropolis?*

### Critical Analytical Synthesis & Direct Mapping

1. **Structural Conflict: High-FAR Revenue Generation vs. Ecological Carrying Capacity**
   - *Paper Findings*: Dai et al. emphasize that the commercial financial viability of Hong Kong and Japanese TOD relies on massive land intensification, yielding extreme Floor Area Ratios (e.g., FAR up to 14.0 in Nihonbashi, PDF p. 6) and developer FAR incentives to offset infrastructure costs (PDF p. 5).
   - *Application to My Research*: In the Northern Metropolis (e.g., Wetland Conservation Areas / Wetland Buffer Areas around Deep Bay and San Tin), the traditional R+P requirement for high-density residential/commercial towers creates severe structural tension. Applying standard R+P financial logic mandates maximal built volume to cross-subsidize railway extension costs, directly violating environmental land-use caps and bird flight corridor protections.

2. **Environmental Limitation: Omission of Natural Ecosystem Services in Classic 3D/5D Metrics**
   - *Paper Findings*: The paper defines TOD sustainability strictly in terms of urban core built environment metrics (3D: Density, Diversity, Design; 5D: Distance, Destination Accessibility) and transport operational decarbonization ($CO_2$, $NO_x$ reductions, Table 5, PDF pp. 4, 8).
   - *Application to My Research*: The traditional R+P / TOD framework treats "greening" merely as urban artificial landscape (e.g., 30,000 m² rooftop/podium greening at West Kowloon, PDF p. 7). It lacks indicators for natural ecosystem functionality, wetland hydrology preservation, water table protection, or habitat fragmentation. Relying on traditional TOD metrics in the Northern Metropolis risks masking severe ecological degradation behind "green building" labels.

3. **Engineering & Civil Cost Bottlenecks in Sensitive Hydrology**
   - *Paper Findings*: Table 3 shows that underground tunneling and hub construction incur massive upfront economic costs ($86.7$ million yuan for excavation operations, PDF p. 7), justified in West Kowloon by urban central land scarcity.
   - *Application to My Research*: In wetland ecosystems, tunneling or underground rail construction requires intensive dewatering, piling, and ground stabilization. This civil engineering approach risks permanently altering sub-surface wetland hydrology, disrupting fishpond networks, and increasing civil costs exponentially compared to conventional urban site developments.

4. **Equity and Stakeholder Governance Gaps**
   - *Paper Findings*: Dai et al. cite social equity loss, land tenure complexity, and developer profit-seeking as key TOD challenges (PDF pp. 3–4).
   - *Application to My Research*: The traditional R+P framework privileges the MTR Corporation and private developers in top-down land assembly. When deployed in the Northern Metropolis, this financialized model risks marginalizing local indigenous villagers, eco-tourist operators, and conservation groups who prioritize non-monetized ecological land values over commercial FAR capture.
